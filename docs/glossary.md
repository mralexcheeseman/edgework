# Glossary

Every name, idea and piece of jargon used in the Edgework docs and apps, explained so that someone new can follow along. Entries are grouped by topic and alphabetical within each group. Where it helps, an entry ends with **Go deeper**: where to read more.

## The project

**Alex Cheeseman.** The writer behind all of this. He writes, from the UK and from years inside large companies, about how AI is changing marketing, work and judgement. Everything published here appears under his name.

**Edgework.** Alex's publication and the name of the whole system: the private newsroom, the public site and the design. The word comes from the sociologist Stephen Lyng, who used "edgework" in 1990 for voluntary risk-taking: deliberately going near the edge to test yourself. Lyng took the idea from Hunter S. Thompson's writing about "the Edge". For Alex it means writing that takes a position and risks being wrong.
*Go deeper:* Stephen Lyng, "Edgework: A Social Psychological Analysis of Voluntary Risk Taking", *American Journal of Sociology* (1990); Hunter S. Thompson, *Hell's Angels* (1966), the closing pages.

**Edgework site.** The public website ([edge-work-vault.base44.app](https://edge-work-vault.base44.app)). It holds Alex's Positions, his essays (linked out to The Last Animal), short Dispatches, his Work and an About page. It's private until Alex approves it for launch.

**Hunter S. Thompson.** American journalist (1937–2005) who invented "gonzo" journalism: first-person reporting where the writer is part of the story, written fast, often overnight, under pressure. Edgework borrows his attitude and imagery (the manuscript filed at 4am, red ink, coffee stains), not his style.
*Go deeper:* *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* (1971); *The Great Shark Hunt* (1979), a collection of his journalism.

**Owl Farm.** Alex's private newsroom app ([owl-farm-notes.base44.app](https://owl-farm-notes.base44.app), login only). It's named after Hunter S. Thompson's home in Woody Creek, Colorado, where he wrote much of his work. It holds the Wire, the Desk, the Typewriter, Cut-up, the Archive, Positions and Thinkers.

**The Last Animal.** Alex's essay site ([thelastanimal.base44.app](https://thelastanimal.base44.app)), with 37 long essays published between March and July 2026. Each Position is argued in full in one of these essays. The Edgework site links to them rather than copying them, so each essay has one home.

## Ideas inside the system

**Arena.** The three subjects every Position belongs to:
- **The marketing machine:** how brands, media and buying are changing.
- **AI and work:** what happens to jobs, skills and organisations.
- **Judgement:** how people and institutions decide, trust and understand.

**Collision.** What the Wire files when a newly published piece meets one of Alex's Positions. Every collision has a relation: the piece **supports** the Position (agrees with it), **challenges** it (disagrees) or **extends** it (takes it somewhere new). Collisions that challenge are usually the best to write from, because disagreement creates tension.

**Conviction.** How sure Alex is of a Position, from 1 (a hunch) to 5 (would defend it anywhere). It's shown as filled dots on the Edgework site and helps rank collisions.

**Core claim.** The single main argument of a piece the Wire has read, written in one sentence so Alex can judge it fast.

**Lens.** A thinker whose way of seeing is applied to a draft. The default lens is the writer of the piece being responded to. A contrast lens adds tension; for example, Byron Sharp (who argues brand loyalty is mostly a myth) set against a piece about loyalty. A lens shapes the argument; the draft never imitates the lens or quotes them unless the material does.

**Lines worth stealing / highlights.** Sentences taken word for word from a piece. The Wire suggests some, and Alex can add his own. They are the only quotes a draft is allowed to use, which keeps every quote real.

**Position.** One of the 30 things Alex believes, each stated in a sentence or two and argued in full in a Last Animal essay. Example: *"AI usage metrics are the McNamara fallacy: measurable, and beside the point"* (Position #22, from the essay "The McNamara Fallacy and the AI Usage OKR Trap"). The master list is the Positions ledger in Notion. Positions marked "edit" are due a rewrite.

**Reaction.** Alex's own one to three sentences on a collision, written on the Desk before anything is drafted. It's the spine of every draft; without it there's nothing worth writing.

**Spark.** A short note of Alex's own: an idea, a line, an observation. Sparks can be drafted from just like collisions. Some come from Cut-up.

**Synopsis.** A short summary of a piece the Wire has read in full, so Alex can decide without opening it.

**Thinker.** One of the 50 writers and researchers Alex follows. Tier 1 are the most important to him. Only 17 have a free feed the Wire can read; the rest can still be chosen as lenses. See [`sources.md`](sources.md).

## The daily tools

**Archive.** Every draft, grouped by status: idea, drafted, edited, exported, published.

**Cut-up.** A Friday exercise: Owl Farm cuts sentences from Alex's writing and his thinkers' writing into strips and shuffles them, to throw up unexpected combinations. The technique comes from the painter Brion Gysin and the novelist William S. Burroughs, who cut up printed text with a razor in Paris in 1959 to find new meanings. David Bowie later used it to write lyrics. Any surprising line can be saved as a Spark.
*Go deeper:* William S. Burroughs and Brion Gysin, *The Third Mind* (1978).

**The Desk.** Where a collision or Spark becomes a draft ([/desk](https://owl-farm-notes.base44.app/desk)). There are four steps: pick the material, read the briefing, write your reaction, choose a shape. Then two AI passes run:
- the **writer pass** plans an angle and writes a first draft in Alex's voice;
- the **editor pass** scores that draft on 11 checks and rewrites anything that fails.

Drafting uses Claude Opus 5.5, an AI model made by Anthropic, run through Base44 and paid from Base44 credits.

**The Typewriter.** Turns a draft into an image to post ([/typewriter](https://owl-farm-notes.base44.app/typewriter)): typewritten text on paper with real scanned coffee stains, tape and red pen. Sizes are 1080 × 1350 (a portrait post) and 1080 × 1920 (a phone-screen reel).

**The pick.** The one collision the Telegram bot sends each morning for Alex to react to by replying. It's the newest collision he hasn't seen yet; **↪ Another one** moves to the next.

**The Sunday letter.** A weekly email to readers, built by Owl Farm every Sunday from the pieces Alex published that week. Owl Farm writes it; Alex pastes it into **Beehiiv** (the newsletter platform at [beehiiv.com](https://www.beehiiv.com)) and sends it. A list of email readers is an audience he owns, unlike followers on a social network.

**The Wire.** Owl Farm's home page and the daily reading machine. Every day at 6am (London) it reads new, free-to-read pieces from Alex's thinkers, keeps only those it can read in full, and files collisions with his Positions: at most 10 a day, and only strong matches.

## Formats (the shapes a draft can take)

**Dispatch.** A short column of 250–400 words: a scene, the real issue, Alex's position, one piece of evidence, what it means this week, and a closing line that echoes the opening. The name comes from a newspaper dispatch: a report filed from somewhere.

**Essay.** 800–1,200 words, usually written on Fridays from the week's best idea. Essays are published on The Last Animal.

**Expense report.** The argument told as four to six "line items" that a mistake has cost an organisation, each with a dry cost, then a total that delivers the verdict. Example line: *"Quarterly AI dashboard: six weeks, no answer."*

**Hey Rube.** One provocation of at most 25 words, the quickest format for a busy day. The name is Hunter S. Thompson's: "Hey Rube" was the title of his column for ESPN.com (2000–2005) and of a 2004 collection of it. The phrase is old American carnival slang, the shout carnival workers used to call each other for help in a fight with locals (the "rubes", or easy marks). Here it means a short, sharp jab at a lazy belief.
*Go deeper:* Hunter S. Thompson, *Hey Rube: Blood Sport, the Bush Doctrine, and the Downward Spiral of Dumbness* (2004).

**Routine.** A 60–90 second script, 150–220 words, written to be said out loud: for a reel, a talk or a podcast. "Routine" as in a comedian's rehearsed set.

## Writing terms

**Callback.** Ending a piece by returning to its opening image, now with new meaning. One of Alex's signature moves: *"So. Back to the corner. 2am."*

**Caption.** The text posted with a card on LinkedIn or Instagram. It has to make sense to a stranger scrolling past, credit the source by name and say who they are. The Desk writes one and the Copy button adds the first Go deeper link.

**Deck.** The one sentence under a headline that says what the piece is about (in British newspapers, a "standfirst").

**Enemy.** The false belief or lazy habit a piece argues against. Every good draft names one.

**Flourish.** One of six personality moves (a sensory detail, an outrageous comparison, a bracketed aside, repetition, a confession, dry understatement). A draft uses two or three. See [`craft-playbook.md`](craft-playbook.md).

**Go deeper.** Two to four links under every piece for readers who want more: the piece it responds to, Alex's own essay on the Position, and the writer's or lens thinker's wider work, each with a line on what you'll get from it. Links are picked only from real addresses Owl Farm already holds, never invented. "Look up" lines name ideas to search for.

**Pull line.** The one sentence people would repeat, set large on the card. *Asterisks* in the text mark the two to four words that get the highlighter.

**Read-alone rule.** Every piece must make sense to someone who has never seen the source, Owl Farm or Alex's Positions. That means:
- the source is named and introduced;
- every person, idea and term is explained on first mention;
- nothing refers to things the reader can't see ("Position #22", "the Wire", "this piece").

The Desk flags any such reference in red.

**Two-beat reframe.** The old belief, then the sharper picture, in two short sentences. *"Trust didn't disappear. It went sideways."*

## Ideas that come up in the Positions

**Aggregation theory.** Ben Thompson's idea that on the internet, whoever owns the relationship with the customer (Google, Amazon, Meta) wins, because suppliers have to come to them.
*Go deeper:* [stratechery.com](https://stratechery.com), "Aggregation Theory" (2015).

**Goodhart's law.** "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Named after the British economist Charles Goodhart (1975); that wording is the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's.

**McNamara fallacy.** Trusting only what can be counted and ignoring what can't. Named after Robert McNamara, the US defence secretary who judged progress in the Vietnam war by enemy body counts. The social scientist Daniel Yankelovich described it in 1972. Alex uses it against AI dashboards that count prompts and seats instead of better work (Position #22).
*Go deeper:* Alex's essay [The McNamara Fallacy and the AI Usage OKR Trap](https://thelastanimal.base44.app/essays/mcnamara-fallacy-ai-okr-trap).

**OKR.** Objectives and key results: a goal-setting method where each goal has a few measurable results. Popularised by Intel and then Google.

**Procrustean bed.** In Greek myth, Procrustes stretched or cut his guests to fit his bed. Nassim Nicholas Taleb uses it for forcing messy reality into neat models. See Alex's essay [The Procrustean Bed](https://thelastanimal.base44.app/essays/the-procrustean-bed-what-happens-when-we-force-living-systems-to-behave-like-machines).

**Zero-click search.** A search that ends without the person clicking any link, because the answer is shown on the results page or given by an AI. It undermines businesses built on search traffic (Position #7).

## The machinery

**Base44.** The platform both apps are built and hosted on ([base44.com](https://base44.com)). Apps there have:
- **entities:** database tables, such as Position and Draft;
- **backend functions:** small server programs, such as `generateDraft` and `dailyWire`;
- **workflows:** scheduled jobs, such as "The 6am Wire";
- **secrets:** private keys stored outside the code;
- **checkpoints:** saved versions you can restore.

**Daily nudge.** The reminder system. A Base44 workflow runs the `dailyNudge` function at 07:30, 12:30, 18:30 and 21:30 (London). It checks what's been done and messages Alex on Telegram and email about what's still open.

**Design system.** The rules for how Edgework looks: colours, fonts, spacing, components and the library of scanned marks. It lives in [`mralexcheeseman/design`](https://github.com/mralexcheeseman/design/tree/main/brands/edgework) under `brands/edgework`. **Tokens** are its named values (for example `red` = #c4211a), so every app uses the same ones.

**Marks.** Real scans of coffee rings, splashes, burns, tape and highlighter, with the paper removed so they sit on any card. They're never drawn or generated as stains.

**Kept share.** How much of the Desk's wording survived Alex's edit: the share of the draft's original words still in the version he kept. Shown on every edited draft and in the Sunday message. Rising over time means the Desk is learning his voice.

**Readwise.** An app that collects the passages Alex highlights in books, articles and podcasts ([readwise.io](https://readwise.io)). The Wire reads his new highlights every morning, before the feeds, because a line he chose is a stronger signal than anything a feed sends.

**Reach and reactions.** What a posted card did a week later. Reach is impressions or views; reactions are likes, comments, reposts and saves added together. Alex records both in the draft, under "After posting", so reviews can compare Positions and shapes by what readers did.

**Streak.** The number of days in a row Alex finished all three daily steps (a card exported or a draft published).

**Telegram bot.** [@Acowlfarm_bot](https://t.me/Acowlfarm_bot): the account that sends Alex his reminders on Telegram.

**Voice rules.** Instructions the Desk learned from how Alex edits its drafts. Every Sunday the `editLessons` function compares the Desk's version of each draft with the one he kept and proposes up to four rules; he adopts or rejects each in Telegram. Adopted rules go into every new draft and override the general voice notes where they disagree.

**Webhook.** The way Telegram passes Alex's replies to Owl Farm: Telegram calls the `telegramHook` function each time he writes to the bot, signed with a secret so nobody else can.

**TT2020.** A free typewriter font in which no two letters print quite the same, like a real typewriter. Edgework uses it for everything meant to be read.
